## Course recap

Title: Useful Introduction formula review

Description: Use this when meeting a new colleague so they quickly understand who you are, how your work connects, and what makes collaboration easier.

Formula:

1. Who you are — Give your name, team, role, or project connection.
2. What you do — Describe the part of your work that is most relevant to this person.
3. How you collaborate — Name one useful preference or practical next step.

Final checklist:

- The other person can place me in the work.
- I gave relevant context instead of my full background.
- My warmth matches the setting.
- I named a preference without apologizing for it.
- I made the next point of collaboration easier.

## Slide 1: What a Work Introduction Needs to Do

A useful introduction is not a performance or a compressed résumé. It gives a new colleague enough context to understand where you fit, what they can come to you for, and how to take the next step with you.

### The goal

- Help the other person place you in the work.
- Give them one useful point of connection.
- Make the next interaction easier than the first.

### What you do not owe

- Your full work history.
- An apology for your communication preferences.
- Personal or diagnostic information you do not want to share.

### What changes by setting

- Slack can be short and conversational.
- Email usually needs a clear reason for reaching out.
- A live introduction can be one or two sentences, followed by a question.

## Slide 2: Strengths You Bring to Work

Neurodivergent people are often told — implicitly or directly — that the way their brain works is a problem to be managed. At work, that narrative can follow you into every meeting, every email, and every introduction. But this is not true. The traits that have been framed as too much, too intense, too detail-oriented, or too blunt are often exactly what makes neurodivergent people exceptional colleagues. You do not need to hide them or qualify them. Select the strengths that feel true for you.

Question: Which strengths do you want a new colleague to understand about you?

Options:

- Organizing messy information
- Spotting user confusion
- Deep focus
- Direct communication
- Creative problem-solving
- Careful follow-through
- Other

## Slide 3: Some Common Mistakes

Most introduction mistakes do not come from saying the wrong thing — they come from a habit of making yourself smaller before you have even started. Over-explaining, softening, or leaving out the most useful context are all ways of stepping back when stepping forward would serve you better. These cards show the patterns that get in the way most often and what to do instead.

### Too vague

The other person does not know what you do or why you are reaching out.

### Too apologetic

You spend more time softening yourself than giving useful context.

### Too much personal context

You disclose things that do not need to be part of a first work intro.

## Slide 4: Warmth Without Overdoing It

Instruction: Knowing the patterns is different from seeing them. Sort each line below by what it actually signals to the other person — and notice how small differences in wording can change the whole impression.

Sort into: Too Vague, Too Much Personal Context, or Balanced.

1. “I work on onboarding.”
   - Too Vague
   - Technically useful, but not very connective.
2. “I promise I am easy to work with once you get to know me!”
   - Too Much Personal Context
   - It asks the other person to reassure you before they know you.
3. “I am excited to work together and can help with the onboarding flow context.”
   - Balanced
   - Warm and useful without overselling.
4. “Hi, I am Alex from product. Let me know if you need anything.”
   - Too Vague
   - It is polite, but it does not explain what Alex owns or what kind of help they can offer.
5. “I get overwhelmed in meetings, forget verbal instructions, and have had problems with coworkers misunderstanding me.”
   - Too Much Personal Context
   - Those experiences may be real, but a first introduction does not require this level of disclosure.
6. “I own the first-run onboarding flow, so I can help with the background behind the current decisions.”
   - Balanced
   - It gives specific, relevant context and makes collaboration easier.
7. “Sorry in advance — I ask a lot of questions and can be kind of intense about details.”
   - Too Much Personal Context
   - This frames a potentially useful preference as a warning and asks the colleague to judge it before working together.
8. “Written next steps help me stay aligned, and I am happy to send a recap after we connect.”
   - Balanced
   - It names a useful preference and shows how it benefits both people.
9. “I am around if needed.”
   - Too Vague
   - The colleague still has to guess what the person can help with.

## Slide 5: Adapt the Same Introduction to the Setting

The core information can stay the same while the length and next step change. You do not need a different personality for every channel.

### Slack

- Keep it conversational and easy to scan.
- Name the project connection and offer one practical resource or next step.

### Email

- Use a clear reason for reaching out.
- Include enough context that the message makes sense when read later.

### Live meeting

- Use one or two sentences, then leave room for the other person.
- A simple question can turn the introduction into a conversation.

### Personal disclosure

- Share only what you want and what is useful for this working relationship.
- You can name a collaboration preference without explaining a diagnosis or personal history.

## Slide 6: The Formula to a Good Introduction

A good introduction does not need to be impressive — it just needs to give the other person enough to work with. When you know what each part is doing and why it is there, the whole thing feels less like a performance and more like a conversation.

1. Who you are — A name and team gives the other person somewhere to place you. Example: “Hi, I am Alex. I am on the product team.”
2. What you do — Name the work you do day to day and how it connects to their world. Example: “I write the help docs users see when they get stuck.”
3. How you collaborate — Name one useful preference or next step. Example: “Written next steps help me stay aligned.”

## Slide 7: Step 1 — Who You Are

Most people rush past Step 1 without thinking about what it is actually doing. A name and team is not just a formality — it is the foundation everything else builds on. Without it the other person has no frame of reference for anything you say next. This step is about giving them somewhere to place you before you ask them to take in anything else.

### What to include

- Your first name.
- Your role or title, kept simple.
- The team or project you are connected to.
- How long you have been there if it is relevant.

### What to leave out

- Your full job history.
- Qualifications unless they directly matter.
- Anything that sounds like you are justifying your presence.
- Over-explaining why you are reaching out before saying who you are.

### Why it can feel hard

- A pull to over-explain.
- Uncertainty about formality.
- Not knowing where Step 1 ends.
- Defaulting to credentials instead of connection-relevant information.

### Tone

- Warm and direct works in most professional contexts.
- Overly formal can create distance.
- Overly casual before you know the person can land wrong.

## Slide 8: Step 2 — What You Do

Knowing your name and team tells the other person who you are. Knowing what you actually do tells them how you fit into their world and whether your work will intersect with theirs. Without this step the introduction stays surface level — polite but not particularly useful to either person.

### What to include

- The kind of work you do day to day.
- The problem your work solves or the thing you move forward.
- How your work connects to theirs if that is already clear.
- One specific current project if it is relevant.

### What to leave out

- A full list of responsibilities.
- Technical language or acronyms they may not know yet.
- Anything that sounds like you are proving your value.

### Why it can feel hard

- Underselling with something vague.
- Overselling by listing every responsibility.
- Not knowing which part is most relevant.
- Defaulting to a title instead of describing the work.

### A note on specificity

- Specificity gives the other person something to respond to.
- “I write the help docs users see when they get stuck” is easier to connect with than “I work on content.”

## Slide 9: Step 3 — How Do You Like to Collaborate?

How you work best with other people is not something you need to hide or apologize for. Neurodivergent people often have very specific and very reasonable preferences around communication — needing things in writing, preferring async over real-time, wanting clear expectations before starting. These are not high-maintenance requests. They are the conditions under which you do your best work.

### What to include

- One specific preference around how you communicate best.
- One preference around how you receive information.
- A simple next step if one is relevant.
- A framing that makes the preference sound useful rather than limiting.

### What to leave out

- An apology before or after naming the preference.
- An explanation of why the preference exists.
- More than one preference at a time.
- Anything that frames the preference as a problem.

### Why it matters

- Naming a preference is an act of clarity, not a request for special treatment.
- The other person benefits from knowing how to work with you well — and so do you.

### Why it can feel hard

- Worrying that naming a preference will make you seem difficult.
- Not knowing which preference to name first.
- Leaving the other person to figure it out through trial and error.
- Framing the preference around what does not work rather than what does.

## Slide 10: Translate the Preference

There is usually a gap between how you naturally think about your collaboration preferences and how you describe them to someone new. The honest version in your head is real and valid — it just needs a small translation to land well in a work introduction.

Instruction: Tap an initial thought on the left, then tap the work version on the right that says the same thing.

### Processing time

Initial thought: “I freeze when people ask me questions live.”

Work version: “I usually give better answers when I have a few minutes to think — I will always follow up in writing.”

Mismatch feedback: Look for the version that makes processing time sound useful and professional rather than like a limitation.

### Directness

Initial thought: “Please do not make me guess what you mean.”

Work version: “Direct feedback is easiest for me to act on, especially when the priority is clear.”

Mismatch feedback: Look for the version that asks for direct feedback without sounding frustrated or demanding.

### Context

Initial thought: “I need the whole story or I will miss something.”

Work version: “A little context up front helps me avoid rework and move faster.”

Mismatch feedback: Look for the version that frames the need for context as a benefit to the work rather than a personal requirement.

### Interruptions

Initial thought: “I hate being interrupted mid-thought.”

Work version: “I tend to do my best thinking when I can finish a thought before we discuss — async works really well for me.”

Mismatch feedback: Look for the version that names the preference around interruptions without sounding defensive or difficult to work with.

### Written steps

Initial thought: “I need things written down or I will forget.”

Work version: “Written next steps help me stay aligned — I am happy to send a recap after we connect.”

Mismatch feedback: Look for the version that makes written confirmation sound like something that benefits both people.

## Slide 11: Choose the Most Useful Introduction

Put the full formula together. Choose the introduction that gives the other person enough context, matches the setting, and makes collaboration easier without overexplaining.

Checklist:

- Who you are.
- What you do that is relevant here.
- One useful preference, resource, or next step.

### Round 1

Scenario: A new cross-functional colleague messages you in Slack before reading the project document.

- A: “Hi! I am Alex from product. I own the first-run onboarding flow, so I can help with context behind the current decisions. Happy to point you to the most useful sections of the doc.” Correct. Warm, relevant, and offers a concrete next step.
- B: “Hi. Welcome.” Incorrect. Friendly but gives the colleague no useful context.
- C: “I have been here for four years and have worked across six different projects, so I can tell you everything that has ever happened with onboarding.” Incorrect. Overloads the first interaction with background the colleague does not need yet.

### Round 2

Scenario: You are introduced during a live project kickoff and have about 20 seconds.

- A: “I am Alex from product. I focus on onboarding clarity and will be your partner for the first-run flow. I would love to hear which part you are jumping into first.” Correct. Brief, relevant, and opens the door to collaboration.
- B: “I should warn you that I process things differently and sometimes need people to repeat themselves.” Incorrect. Leads with a warning and more personal context than this moment requires.
- C: “I work here too.” Incorrect. Does not help the colleague understand the connection.

### Round 3

Scenario: You want to name a collaboration preference in a first email.

- A: “Written next steps help me stay aligned, and I am happy to send a recap after we meet.” Correct. Names the preference confidently and shows how it supports the work.
- B: “Sorry, I am difficult about meetings and need everything written down.” Incorrect. Apologizes and frames the preference as a personal problem.
- C: “You will figure out how I work eventually.” Incorrect. Leaves the other person to guess instead of making collaboration easier.

Goal: Introduce yourself, give Maya useful context, and make the next collaboration step clear.

Practice reminders:

- Say who you are and how you connect to the project.
- Name the part of the work most relevant to Maya.
- Share one useful strength, preference, or resource.
- Offer a clear next step or invite a practical question.
- Keep personal disclosure intentional and work-relevant.

What you already know:

- Jordan told her you will both support the beta onboarding work.

- Role or team shared

### Internal practice behavior

Maya is a friendly but busy cross-functional colleague in Slack who has just joined a beta onboarding project.

If the user introduces themselves clearly, Maya responds warmly and asks one practical follow-up. If the user is vague, Maya asks what part of the project they own. If the user overshares or apologizes heavily, Maya stays kind and redirects to collaboration. Maya never breaks character.
